What Arize is

Arize is an AI observability platform — built around its open-source project, Arize Phoenix — for tracing and monitoring AI applications. When your app calls a language model, Arize records the whole interaction as a trace: the prompt that went in, the documents retrieved, the tools called, the answer that came back, how long each step took, and what it cost. Those traces land in a dashboard where you can search them, filter them, and open any single interaction to see the full chain. It is, in short, the flight recorder for an AI app.

That matters because AI apps fail differently from normal software. A normal app fails with an error message and a stack trace. An AI app fails silently: it returns a confident, fluent, wrong answer, and nothing in your logs explains why. Without observability you are left guessing — was the prompt bad, the retrieval wrong, the model confused, or the data stale? With Arize you open the trace and see the answer. For anyone shipping AI to real users, that difference is the difference between fixing a bug in ten minutes and losing a customer you never knew you lost.

Tracing, evals and monitoring, in plain words

Arize's three core ideas sound technical, but each one is simple. Tracing is the flight recorder: every interaction with the AI is saved as a linked chain — prompt, retrieval, tool calls, answer — so you can open one bad answer and see every step that produced it. Evals are automatic quality checks: you define what a good answer looks like — grounded in your documents, polite, complete — and Arize scores your app's answers against that standard, continuously, instead of you reading samples by hand. Monitoring is the dashboard over time: answer quality, response speed, error rates and cost, so you can see a problem developing — a model getting slower, quality slipping after a prompt change — before users complain.

Used together, the three answer the three questions every AI builder asks: what happened, was it good, and is it getting worse? Without them you have opinions. With them you have evidence — and evidence is what lets you improve a prompt, fix a retrieval bug, or justify switching models to a client or a manager.

Arize vs logging it yourself

Every developer's first instinct is to log the AI calls themselves — print the prompt and the answer to a file or a database. That works for a week, then it falls apart. Logs are flat: they record that something happened, but they do not link the prompt to the retrieval to the tool calls to the answer, so reconstructing one bad interaction means grepping through thousands of lines. Logs have no scoring, so you cannot tell whether quality is improving or slipping. And logs have no dashboard, so the only way to check on your app is to read them by hand.

Arize does all three things a log cannot: linked traces you can open, automatic evals that score quality, and a dashboard that shows trends. Building that yourself would take weeks and you would still maintain it forever — or you can buy a year of Arize Pro for Rs 6,650 and spend the time improving your app instead. The same logic applies to the rest of the stack: n8n Starter automates the workflows around your app, and Resend Pro handles its email — buy the specialist tool for each job instead of building all of them badly yourself.

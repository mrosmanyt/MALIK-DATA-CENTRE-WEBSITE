What Resend is

Resend is an email API for developers. Instead of your app sending mail through Gmail or a server you configured yourself, your app calls Resend's API and Resend delivers the email — from your own domain, with proper authentication, tracking and a dashboard that shows what was delivered, opened and bounced. It is the tool behind the "welcome to the app" and "your order is confirmed" emails of thousands of modern startups, and it has become the default choice for developers building with Next.js, React and other modern stacks.

The key idea is separation. Your app should do your app's job — sign-ups, orders, notifications — and a specialist service should do email. When email lives inside your app, every deliverability problem becomes your problem: spam filters, blacklists, rate limits, bounced addresses. When it lives in Resend, those problems belong to a service whose entire business is solving them. That is why even small apps use it: the cost of one lost customer who never received a verification email is higher than a year of Resend Pro.

What Resend Pro gives you

Resend Pro is the paid tier, and it gives you the things the free tier cannot: higher sending limits for a real user base, your own sending domain with full authentication, and the dashboard and analytics that show whether your mail is actually landing. You can see every send — delivered, bounced, opened, clicked — and debug a missing email in minutes instead of guessing. For a developer, that visibility is the real product: an email you cannot trace is an email you cannot fix.

Pro also covers the professional details that matter once real users are involved: DKIM and SPF authentication so receiving servers trust your mail, dedicated sending setup for higher volumes, and support when something goes wrong. If your app sends a handful of emails a month, the free tier is enough and you should not buy Pro. If it sends hundreds or thousands — sign-ups, orders, notifications — Pro is the point where email stops being a risk and starts being infrastructure.

Who needs Resend

SaaS builders are the clearest buyers. Any app with sign-ups needs verification emails, password resets and notifications, and those emails have to arrive every time. Online shops are the second group: order confirmations, shipping updates and receipts are transactional email, and a shop whose order emails go to spam gets support messages asking "did my order go through?" — which costs more in time than Resend Pro costs in a year. Agencies and freelancers who build sites and apps for clients are the third group: Resend is the professional answer when a client asks "how will our app send email?", and it is far more credible than "we will use a Gmail account". Developers learning modern stacks are the fourth: Resend is the email service the Next.js and Vercel ecosystem standardises on, so learning it is learning the industry default.

Who does not need it? Anyone sending only newsletters or bulk marketing mail — that is a different job, and a marketing email service fits it better. And anyone whose app sends almost no email yet should wait until the volume justifies Pro.

Resend vs Gmail and your own server

The comparison most developers make is with the free options, so here it is honestly. Gmail is not built for app email: it caps how much you can send per day, it flags automated mail, and an email from a Gmail address looks unprofessional to users and untrustworthy to spam filters. Your own server — the SMTP setup on a VPS — works until it does not: one misconfigured record and your mail goes to spam for every user, and debugging deliverability from a server you manage yourself is a full-time job. Resend costs Rs 15,850 a year and removes the entire problem: authenticated sending from your domain, visible delivery, and a service whose reputation with Gmail and Outlook is already established.

Think of it the way you think about hosting. You could run your own server, but you use Vercel or a host instead, because the specialist does it better and cheaper than your time is worth. Email is the same: the day your app has real users, the free options cost more in lost users and debugging time than Resend Pro costs in a year.

Setting up Resend in an afternoon

The setup is deliberately short, and it is the same for almost every app. First, verify your domain: Resend gives you a few DNS records to add where your domain lives — this proves you own the domain and is what keeps your mail out of spam. Second, create an API key in the Resend dashboard; this is the password your app uses to talk to Resend. Third, send your first email: in a Next.js app that is a few lines of code with Resend's SDK, and in Laravel or Python it is a single API call. Fourth, check the dashboard to confirm the email was delivered, and you are done — the same code then sends every sign-up, receipt and alert your app will ever need.

What is transactional email?

Transactional email is mail your app sends because something happened: a user signed up, an order was placed, a password was reset, a payment failed. It is different from marketing email, and it has to arrive — a missing password-reset email means a lost user.

Do I need my own domain to use Resend?

Yes, for real use. Resend sends from your domain, which is what makes the email trusted and keeps it out of spam. You verify the domain once with a few DNS records, then every email your app sends carries your brand instead of a generic address.

Does Resend work with Next.js, Laravel and WordPress?

Yes. Resend has official SDKs for JavaScript and TypeScript, so Next.js apps connect in minutes, and it also works over a standard API from Laravel, Python, or any backend that can make an HTTP request. WordPress sites usually connect through an SMTP plugin.
